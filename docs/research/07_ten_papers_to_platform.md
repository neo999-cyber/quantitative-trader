# Ten classic papers → where each one lives in our platform

The social-media list is a fair reading list. What matters for us is not the history but the *one operational idea* each paper contributes, and where it is wired into the plan. Papers already embedded in `PLAN.md` / `04_statistical_rigor.md` are marked ✔; the rest get a specific home below.

| # | Paper | The one idea | Where it lives in our platform | What it does **not** say |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Markowitz 1952**, Portfolio Selection | Risk is a property of the portfolio, not the position: what matters is covariance. Diversification across low-correlation return streams raises return per unit of risk. | Layer 6 (skfolio / Riskfolio-Lib). Concretely: the trial's four strategy families are scored *jointly*, and the allocation step weights survivors by their covariance, not their individual Sharpe. Also why the plan prefers **many small independent edges** over one big one. ✔ | Mean-variance with estimated inputs is unstable; we use shrinkage (Ledoit-Wolf) and hierarchical risk parity rather than raw MVO. |
| 2 | **Modigliani-Miller 1958** | In a frictionless world capital structure is irrelevant; every real-world deviation (taxes, bankruptcy costs, information) is a friction worth studying. | Mostly not applicable to a trading platform. One use: the *method* — start from a frictionless model, then price each friction explicitly. That is exactly how the cost model is built: gross return, then spread, fees, impact, borrow, funding, each as a separate line. | Nothing about trading. Skip for the trial. |
| 3 | **Sharpe 1964**, CAPM | Expected return is compensation for exposure to the market factor; the rest is either alpha or noise. The Sharpe ratio (return per unit of risk) is the common language. | Every gate that uses Sharpe (3, 4, 7, 9) and every benchmark comparison. Concretely: a strategy's return is always reported *net of its market beta* (BTC beta for crypto, SPY beta for equities); a long-only crypto strategy that is 90% BTC beta has no alpha to validate. Add to the Hypothesis Report: beta to the asset-class index and the alpha t-stat, not just raw Sharpe. ✔ | CAPM as a pricing model is empirically weak; we use it as a *decomposition*, not a forecast. |
| 4 | **Fama 1970**, Efficient Markets | Efficiency is only testable jointly with a model of expected returns (the joint-hypothesis problem). Any "anomaly" could be a mis-specified benchmark. | Gate 5's benchmark set. Concretely: every strategy is tested against *three* benchmarks (buy-and-hold, a random-entry strategy with matched trade count, and a factor-adjusted return), because beating one benchmark can be a benchmark error rather than an edge. ✔ (SPA/StepM vs benchmark) | It does not say markets are perfectly efficient; see #7. |
| 5 | **Black-Scholes 1973** | The price of an option follows from the cost of replicating it with the underlying, not from a forecast of direction. Volatility is the tradeable quantity. | Not used in the crypto-spot trial. Later: (a) the options-flow screen already in `centaur/` should reason in *implied volatility* terms, not raw volume; (b) if we ever hedge a swing position with options (Centaur Rule 4 "hedge through earnings"), the hedge cost is a BS/implied-vol calculation. Also the intellectual root of **volatility targeting** (Layer 6): position size ∝ 1/σ. | Assumes constant vol and continuous hedging; real options need a surface (ORATS/Theta in the serious tier). |
| 6 | **Kelly 1956** | The bet fraction that maximises long-run growth is f* = edge/odds (continuous: μ/σ²). Over-betting beyond f* *reduces* growth and eventually ruins you; half-Kelly keeps 75% of growth at half the variance. | Gate 11. The 1% rule is ≈ quarter-Kelly for a typical system; we size with fractional Kelly on the *deflated, hold-out* Sharpe and keep 1% as the hard cap. Also the reason gates 3–9 exist: Kelly is only safe if the edge estimate is honest — an overestimated edge produces an over-bet. ✔ | Kelly assumes you know the edge. You don't; that is why the fraction is ≤ ½. |
| 7 | **Grossman-Stiglitz 1980** | If prices were fully informative, nobody would pay to gather information, so nobody would; therefore prices must leave *just enough* inefficiency to pay informed traders for their costs. | The platform's business case, and the expected magnitude of edges. Concretely: expect edges roughly the size of the cost of finding and trading them — small, decaying, and concentrated where information or execution is expensive (small caps, crypto alts, event windows). It is why the plan's expected live Sharpe is 0.5–1.0, not 3. ✔ (implicit in the decay numbers) | It does not say inefficiencies are large or persistent. |
| 8 | **Fama-French 1993** (open-access 2004 follow-up) | Cross-sectional returns are explained by a few common factors (market, size, value; later profitability and investment). A strategy's return that loads on these factors is factor exposure, not alpha. | Gate 5 and the Hypothesis Report: regress strategy returns on the relevant factor set (for crypto: market, size, momentum per Liu–Tsyvinski–Wu; for equities: FF5 + momentum from Ken French's free data library) and report *factor-adjusted alpha*. Strategy families 2 and 3 in the trial (cross-sectional momentum, quality) are direct descendants. ✔ | Factor premia themselves decay and have decade-long drawdowns (value 2018–20). |
| 9 | **Jegadeesh-Titman 1993** | Stocks that outperformed over the past 3–12 months keep outperforming for 3–12 months; the effect reverses at 1 month and over 3–5 years. | Trial strategy families 1 and 2 (time-series and cross-sectional momentum) and the weekly-reversal family (the 1-month reversal is the same paper's flip side). Implementation detail that comes straight from the paper: **skip the most recent month** when ranking (the "12-1" convention) to avoid the short-term reversal contaminating the momentum signal. ✔ | Momentum crashes (Daniel–Moskowitz 2016) are why the plan adds crash control (vol scaling, market-state filter). |
| 10 | **Almgren-Chriss 2000** | Execution has a cost that rises with speed (temporary impact) and with size (permanent impact); the optimal schedule trades off impact against the risk of waiting. The backtest-to-P&L gap is mostly this. | Gate 2 (cost survival) and Layer 5. Concretely: the cost model charges half-spread + fees + a square-root impact term Y·σ·√(Q/V); gate 2 requires net ≥ 60% of gross and survival at 2× costs. In Phase 4, NautilusTrader's fill models simulate the participation-rate side. ✔ | The paper's linear-impact model is superseded by the square-root law (Bouchaud); we use the latter with A-C as the framing. |

## Three concrete additions to the plan from this list

1. **Hypothesis Report gains a factor decomposition row** (from #3, #8): beta to the asset-class index, factor loadings, and the *alpha* t-stat. A crypto strategy that is mostly BTC beta fails on alpha even if its raw Sharpe passes gate 3. Implementation: OLS with HAC errors on daily returns against the factor set; free data (Ken French library for equities; construct crypto factors from the lake).
2. **The "12-1" skip-month convention** (from #9) becomes the default for any momentum ranking in the strategy library, and the 1-month reversal is modelled as its own family rather than ignored.
3. **Three benchmarks, not one** (from #4): buy-and-hold, random-entry with matched trade count, and factor-adjusted zero. Already in gate 5's spirit; now explicit.

## Reading order for the trial (optional, ~4 hours)
Kelly (short, 1956) → Jegadeesh-Titman (§I–III only) → Almgren-Chriss (§1–2) → Grossman-Stiglitz (introduction and conclusion). The other six can wait; the plan already carries their conclusions.

## Two papers the list omits that matter more for us
- **Bailey & López de Prado 2014, The Deflated Sharpe Ratio** and **Harvey, Liu & Zhu 2016** — the multiple-testing papers behind gates 4–5. Without these, the ten above produce beautifully overfit strategies.
- **McLean & Pontiff 2016** — post-publication decay, which is why every family in the trial is expected to deliver 40–60% of its paper Sharpe.
